Yes — but not identical. Eritrean and Ethiopian food come from the same culinary tradition: the same sour flatbread, the same spiced stews, the same habit of eating from one shared platter with the right hand. Diners and cooks who know both tend to describe them as close relatives rather than twins, and the differences are the kind you taste rather than read about.

What the two cuisines genuinely share
-------------------------------------

The backbone of both is injera, a large, round, slightly spongy flatbread made from teff, a high-fibre grain. As one account of Eritrean home cooking puts it, injera is the staple food for Eritreans and Ethiopians alike [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/lifeandstyle/2009/jun/17/hannah-pool-eritrean-food). Because it is naturally fermented, injera carries a sour tang that offsets rich, slow-cooked stews — which is exactly why it works as both bread and plate.

The second shared pillar is berberè, the deep red chilli-and-spice blend that gives the stews their colour and heat. Beyond that, the template is the same: portions arrive on a wide platter lined with injera, diners tear off pieces and scoop, and no one gets an individual plate. Anyone who has eaten one cuisine will recognise the other instantly — a point repeatedly made by people familiar with both, who describe the two as quite similar [Cooking Stack Exchange](https://cooking.stackexchange.com/questions/7097/what-are-the-primary-differences-between-ethiopian-and-eritrean-food). Even the everyday supply chain is shared: it is common for Ethiopian and Eritrean households alike to buy their injera rather than make it at home [Taste Wars](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MlNKM61HhN0).

Where Eritrean and Ethiopian cooking pull apart
-----------------------------------------------

### Seafood and the Red Sea

The single most reliable difference is the coastline. Eritrea spent decades as part of Ethiopia before independence, and its cooking absorbed the fish and shellfish of the Red Sea. As the reference literature summarises it, Eritrean cuisine strongly resembles that of neighbouring Ethiopia, "although Eritrean cooking tends to feature more seafood than Ethiopian cuisine" [Eritrean cuisine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eritrean_cuisine). In practice: expect more fish dishes, and expect them further north in the country and along the coast.

### Spice blends and regional accents

Both kitchens use berberè, and both have a hotter, leaner chilli blend for grilled meat, but the recipes are household and regional property rather than national standards. In Eritrea, the inland highlands season differently from the coastal lowlands, and every family defends its own blend. The cuisines are described as quite similar aside from the country's separate national story and its own regional variations [Veganism in Ethiopian \& Eritrean Cuisine](https://goingoutvegan.wordpress.com/2019/02/09/veganism-in-ethiopian-eritrean-cuisine/).

### Names, history and the table

Eritrea's separation from Ethiopia after a long independence war means the same dish can appear under different names on either side of the border. The cooking did not change at the border; the language of the menu did. That historical split is why Eritrean restaurants in Europe often present themselves as Eritrean *and* Ethiopian — the kitchen and the menu overlap too heavily to separate cleanly.

Running the comparison over a single meal
-----------------------------------------

The clearest test is a shared platter. Take a stew of chicken or beef in red berberè, add a lentil or split-pea purée, a fold of collard greens, a cool spoon of spiced cottage cheese, and finish with a salad of tomato and chilli. That combination reads as national cooking on both sides, and it is how most first-timers encounter the food [r/AskAnAmerican](https://www.reddit.com/r/AskAnAmerican/comments/130kwig/what_do_you_think_of_ethiopianeritrean_food/). Where the two diverge at the table is at the edges of the platter: an Eritrean spread more readily includes fish, and Ethiopian menus lean harder on the vegetarian pulse dishes that made the cuisine famous among plant-based eaters.

Testing it in Milan
-------------------

You do not need a flight to Asmara to run this comparison; a single dinner in Porta Venezia can cover it. At Al Sicomoro, injera is prepared in house through three days of natural teff fermentation, and the berberè is made on site from over twelve toasted and ground spices — the two variables most responsible for the difference between an indifferent platter and a good one. Because the same kitchen serves both traditions, dishes appear side by side: Zighinì di carne at €17, Kitfo at €18, a vegetarian Zighinì at €16, with Sambusa di carne at €3 and the vegetable version at €1,50 rounding out the table for sharing.
