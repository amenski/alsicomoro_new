Searches for injera Milano usually lead to Porta Venezia, where a small dining room in the heart of the neighbourhood serves Eritrean and Ethiopian food the way it is meant to be eaten: from a shared platter, by hand, with the flatbread doing the work of plate and cutlery. Here is what injera is, how the meal works, and what to expect before you book.

What injera is, and why it cannot be rushed
-------------------------------------------

Injera is a fermented flatbread made from teff, the tiny grain native to the Ethiopian and Eritrean highlands. The batter is left to ferment naturally, which gives the bread its sour tang and its web of small holes, then baked on a wide griddle into a round large enough to cover a whole tray. At the table it does two jobs at once: it is the utensil — the traditional bread used to lift stews and pulses — and it is also the plate, laid underneath the dishes so that it soaks up sauce as the meal goes on. One Milan menu describes the restaurant as named for "the fermented flatbread that is both plate and utensil" ([Mes Prestiges](https://mesprestiges.com/en/milano/places/ristorante-injera)).

Nothing about that process responds well to shortcuts. The length of the fermentation decides how sour the bread tastes and how open its crumb turns out, which is why a kitchen either commits the days or serves something flatter and blander than it should be.

How the table works
-------------------

Meals are built around sharing. Stews, pulses and vegetables arrive together in the centre of the table rather than in front of each diner, and everyone eats from the same platter with the right hand, tearing a piece of injera and using it to lift a mouthful. Ordering is usually a mix of one or two meat stews and several vegetarian ones, so that textures and heat levels contrast across the tray. Berberè — the toasted spice blend behind much of the colour and heat — runs through a good part of the menu, and a coffee ceremony, poured and served in small cups, is the traditional way to finish. The house berberè blends more than twelve toasted and ground spices, and the coffee ceremony is available on request.

Injera Milano: the Porta Venezia address
----------------------------------------

In Porta Venezia, Al Sicomoro serves Eritrean and Ethiopian cooking at Via Panfilo Castaldi 19, 20124 Milano. It is the same dining room plenty of people still call Injera, since the restaurant was formerly known by that name at the same address — one reason the old search term and the new sign lead to the same tables. The injera is made in house and fermented naturally for three days before it reaches the griddle. The restaurant sits within easy reach of Repubblica, Centrale and Porta Venezia metro stations, according to [its own site](https://www.ristoranteinjera.it/).

The dining room is small, and that is the main reason booking ahead matters more here than in most of the city.

What to order
-------------

Portions are designed for sharing, so the table orders several dishes and everyone eats from the centre. At Al Sicomoro, menu items are priced individually rather than as a set menu:

* Zighinì di carne — €17
* Kitfo — €18
* Zighinì vegetariano — €16
* Fir Fir vegetariano — €15
* Sambusa di carne — €3
* Sambusa di verdura — €1,50
* Categna — €3

Menus of this kind tend to stay short, with [both vegetarian and omnivorous options](https://www.tripadvisor.com/Restaurant_Review-g187849-d11905713-Reviews-Ristorante_Injera-Milan_Lombardy.html) and prices that reviewers consider low for Milan. A vegetarian zighinì with legumes, potatoes, sauerkraut, green beans and carrots is one of the staples of the style, as described in [ViviMilano](https://vivimilano.corriere.it/ristoranti/dal-mondo/ristorante-injera/). If it is your first time, a meat zighinì and a vegetarian one alongside each other cover both ends of the kitchen and give you plenty of injera to carry them.

Before you go: hours, booking and what is not available
-------------------------------------------------------

Al Sicomoro serves lunch and dinner Tuesday to Sunday, from 12:00 to 15:00 and from 18:30 to 23:00, and is closed on Monday. Reservations are taken through TheFork, by phone and on WhatsApp, and they are worth arranging in advance because the dining room is small. The menu is listed on [TheFork](https://www.thefork.com/restaurant/injera-r282873/menu), which is also where bookings can be made. There is no delivery, no takeaway and no online ordering, so the meal happens at the table or not at all. The restaurant also hosts private events.

Why "Injera Milano" still leads here
------------------------------------

Names travel more slowly than signs do. A Facebook page still carrying the Injera name lists [863 followers and 774 check-ins](https://www.facebook.com/YONASINJERA/), which says something about how firmly the old word stuck to the address. The kitchen, the room and the street stayed the same; only the name on the door moved, from Injera to Al Sicomoro. If you are looking for injera in Milan, the table to book is the one at Via Panfilo Castaldi 19.
